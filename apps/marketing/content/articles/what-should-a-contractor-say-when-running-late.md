Even a well-planned day can change. A repair takes longer than expected, traffic stops moving, a supplier is late, or an earlier customer needs more attention than the schedule allowed.

Customers can usually handle a delay. What frustrates them is waiting without an update, especially if they rearranged their workday to be home. The useful response is not a long explanation. It is an early, honest message that says what has changed and what choices they have.

## Send the update as soon as the original plan is unlikely

Do not wait until the end of the arrival window has passed. As soon as the technician or dispatcher can see that the promised window is no longer realistic, contact the customer.

An early message lets the customer decide whether the revised time still works. It also gives your team time to adjust the rest of the day instead of creating several surprises in a row.

Before sending anything, check the facts you actually know:

* The original appointment window
* The current location or stage of the previous job
* A revised arrival range that is genuinely achievable
* Whether another technician, a later visit, or a new day is an option
* Who will make the next update if the situation changes again

Avoid treating a hopeful guess as a confirmed arrival time. If you only know that the team is delayed, say that. Promise a further update at a specific time only when someone is responsible for sending it.

## Keep the first message short and useful

A late-arrival text should identify the business, acknowledge the change, give the best current expectation, and invite a reply. The customer does not need the private details of another job.

For example:

> Hi [First Name], this is [Name] from [Business Name]. Our earlier job is taking longer than expected, so we will not make the original [time] to [time] window. Our best current arrival window is [new window]. Please reply if that no longer works for you, and we will arrange the next option.

If the revised window is not yet clear, use a message that is more cautious:

> Hi [First Name], this is [Name] from [Business Name]. We are delayed at an earlier appointment and will not make the original [time] to [time] window. I will update you by [time] today with a revised arrival option. Sorry for the inconvenience.

The first example gives a clear decision. The second is better than inventing an estimate. Neither needs an excuse longer than the customer can read quickly.

## Offer choices only when you can honour them

A customer may be able to wait, may need to leave, or may prefer another day. If you have a real alternative, make it easy to choose.

> We can still attend between [new window] today, or we can move the visit to [available option]. Please let us know which is better for you.

Do not offer an emergency slot, a discount, or a same-day finish unless it has been approved and can actually be delivered. The purpose of the message is to reset expectations, not to make a second promise that creates more pressure.

When the customer replies, update the calendar and job notes right away. The person driving, the office, and the customer should all be working from the same agreed time. A verbal agreement left only in one person's phone can easily turn into another missed appointment.

## Make the update part of the day's workflow

Late-arrival communication works best when it is not left to memory. A small team can use a simple routine:

1. At the start of each job, compare the expected finish with the next appointment.
2. When a delay is likely, assign one person to contact the affected customer.
3. Record the message, the revised time, and any reply where the team can see it.
4. Check the remaining route and contact the next customer early if the delay will carry forward.
5. Close the loop after the visit with the actual arrival time and any next step.

This is closely connected to sensible scheduling. Service zones, realistic job blocks, and buffers reduce the number of updates you need to send. The guide to [building a schedule that does not waste drive time](/blog/how-can-a-service-business-build-a-schedule-that-does-not-waste-drive-time) explains how to make those decisions before the day starts.

## Do not confuse a new inquiry with an arrival update

Customers waiting for a booked visit need a different response from a caller asking for an estimate. An arrival update should go to the person whose appointment is changing. A new inquiry still needs a clear first response, an owner, and a callback task.

[Never Miss](/never-miss) supports that earlier moment. After an eligible unanswered call, it can send the approved reply your business has set up and create a visible callback task. It does not set appointment times or send late-arrival updates for you. Your team decides the schedule and communicates the details it can honour.

## A clear update protects the relationship

Running late is not always avoidable. Leaving a customer to guess is.

Start with one approved late-arrival template, then make sure the business can see who sent it, what the customer chose, and what is now on the calendar. That small discipline helps the customer plan their day and helps the team recover from a normal field-service problem without losing track of the next promise.
