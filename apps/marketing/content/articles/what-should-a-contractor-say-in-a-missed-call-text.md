When someone calls a contractor, they usually need a quick answer to a practical question: can you help, and when will they hear back?

A missed-call text cannot solve the job over the phone. It can do something smaller and useful: show the caller that their request reached a real business, give them a simple way to explain what they need, and give you enough context for a worthwhile callback.

The best message is short, specific, and honest about what happens next.

## Start with four useful parts

A good missed-call text usually includes:

* Your business name
* A brief acknowledgement that you could not answer
* One simple request for the details you need
* A realistic next step

That structure works because the caller does not need to guess who is texting or whether they should reply. It also keeps you from making a promise you cannot keep while you are on another job.

Here is a straightforward starting point:

> Hi, this is [Business Name]. Sorry we missed your call. Please reply with your name, what you need help with, and your area. We will call you back as soon as we can.

This is not meant to be clever. It tells the customer what to do and gives your team the information needed for the next conversation.

## Make the reply sound like your business

The message should sound like something your business would actually say. A plumbing company, electrician, landscaper, and renovation contractor may all use the same basic structure, but the useful question can change.

For a plumber, you could write:

> Hi, this is [Business Name]. Sorry we missed your call. Please reply with your name, neighbourhood, and a short note about the plumbing issue. We will get back to you as soon as we can.

For an electrician, try:

> Hi, this is [Business Name]. Sorry we could not answer. Please tell us your name, location, and what electrical work you need. We will review your message and call you back.

For a landscaping or maintenance company, a less urgent message may be better:

> Hi, this is [Business Name]. Thanks for calling. Please reply with the property area and the work you have in mind, and we will contact you to discuss the next step.

Only ask for information you will use. A first text is not the right place to ask for a full history, payment information, or a long questionnaire.

## Be careful with timing language

Words such as “right away,” “soon,” and “today” can create an expectation you may not be able to meet. If you are working alone, in a meeting, or booked for the afternoon, a calmer promise is better.

Use a specific timeframe only when you know you can honour it. For example:

> We will call you before 4 p.m. today.

Or, if the office is closed:

> Our team is away from the phone right now. Please reply with the details and we will review it on the next business day.

Do not describe a text as emergency support unless you have a real emergency process and staff ready to act on it. If a caller describes immediate danger, they need the appropriate emergency service, utility, or qualified help, not an ordinary callback queue.

## Keep the question easy to answer

People often reply while standing in a driveway, between meetings, or while dealing with a problem at home. One question is easier than three.

Instead of asking for every detail at once, start with the basics:

> What do you need help with, and which area are you in?

Once they reply, your return call can clarify the scope, timing, site conditions, or photos that would help. Our [contractor lead intake checklist](/blog/contractor-lead-intake-checklist) explains the few details that make that callback useful.

## Do not pretend a person is replying live

An automatic message should still be respectful. It does not need to sound like a bot, but it should not mislead the caller into thinking a dispatcher is actively typing when nobody is available.

“Sorry we missed your call” is clear. “I am checking your schedule now” is not appropriate unless someone truly is.

Similarly, avoid price estimates, diagnosis, availability, or booking promises by text unless the information is accurate for that caller. The goal is to keep the conversation open, not to rush a customer into a decision with incomplete information.

## Include opt-out wording when it belongs in your process

If your business sends an automated recovery text, make the message easy to stop. Use a clear instruction such as “Reply STOP to opt out” when that is part of the message flow, and make sure the team respects it.

That instruction should not take over the message. It can sit naturally at the end:

> Hi, this is [Business Name]. Sorry we missed your call. Reply with your name, the service you need, and your area, and we will call you back. Reply STOP to opt out.

Your exact message and consent process should fit how your business operates. If you are unsure about your obligations, get advice specific to your business rather than copying a message from another company.

## Test the message before relying on it

Send a test from another phone and read it as if you were a new customer. Check that:

* The business name is correct
* The question is easy to understand
* The expected callback timing is honest
* The reply arrives where your team will see it
* Someone owns the next callback
