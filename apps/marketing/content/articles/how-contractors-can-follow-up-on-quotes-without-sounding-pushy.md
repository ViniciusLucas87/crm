Sending a quote is not the end of the conversation. It is the point where the customer needs time, information, and a clear way to respond.

Many contractors avoid following up because they do not want to sound pushy. Others send repeated messages with no new purpose. Neither approach serves the customer particularly well.

A good follow-up process is calm and specific. It makes it easy for someone to ask a question, approve the work, delay it, or tell you they chose another option. It also stops your team from wondering whether anyone has checked in.

## Agree on the next step before you send the quote

The easiest follow-up starts while you are still speaking with the customer.

Before the quote goes out, tell them what to expect. For example: "I will send this by Thursday. Would it be helpful if I checked in next week in case you have questions?"

That single question sets a reasonable expectation. It gives the customer an opportunity to say they are collecting estimates, waiting for an approval, or hoping to start later in the season. Your next message can then match their situation instead of following a generic schedule.

## Make the quote easy to review

Customers often delay because they are busy or because one part of the quote is unclear. A follow-up will not fix a document that is difficult to understand.

Before sending, check that the quote clearly states:

* What work is included and what is not
* The price and any assumptions that affect it
* The expected timing or availability, where you can state it accurately
* How to accept the quote or ask a question
* How long the price or proposed schedule is available, if there is a real limit

Do not invent urgency to create a response. If a supplier price, permit date, or schedule window can genuinely change, explain it plainly. Otherwise, give the customer room to decide.

## Use a short first check-in

For many routine jobs, a brief check-in a few business days after the quote is a sensible starting point. The right timing depends on the work, the customer's stated deadline, and what you agreed during the first conversation.

Try a message like this:

> Hi [Name], this is [Your Name] from [Business Name]. I wanted to make sure you received the estimate for [job]. Is there anything you would like me to clarify before you decide?

This message does not presume the customer is ready to buy. It gives them a low-pressure opening to ask about scope, timing, warranty terms, or an alternative option.

## Follow up with a useful reason, not just a reminder

If there is no reply, your next contact should add context. It might be a note about a question they raised, an upcoming availability window, or a decision needed to hold a site visit that you genuinely can hold.

For example:

> Hi [Name], I am planning next week's schedule and wanted to check whether you would like to move ahead with the [job]. If the timing has changed, just let me know and I can update my notes.

Only mention scheduling when you can honour what you are saying. A customer will notice if every follow-up claims that the calendar is about to fill up.

## Know when to stop

Two respectful attempts are often enough when a customer has not replied and there is no urgent reason to continue. You can close the loop with a final note that leaves the door open:

> Hi [Name], I have not heard back, so I will close this estimate for now. If you would like to revisit the work later, reply here and I will be glad to help if my schedule allows.

This gives the customer control. It also gives your team an honest status instead of a stack of quotes that appear active long after the customer has moved on.

For larger projects, longer decision cycles, or work that depends on another event, agree on a different follow-up date and record the reason. The process should fit the job rather than forcing every customer through the same sequence.

## Keep one record of the conversation

The most common follow-up mistake is not the wording. It is having no reliable record of who last contacted the customer and what was agreed.

After each interaction, record the date, the response, the owner, and the next action. A note such as "customer reviewing with strata, follow up September 12" gives the next person enough context to continue without making the customer repeat themselves.

At low volume, a shared list may be enough. When several people handle calls, texts, and website inquiries, the important requirement is visibility. ## Review the process with your own numbers

Do not assume that more messages will produce better results. For one month, count how many quotes were sent, how many received a first follow-up, how many had a documented next action, and how many customers replied. Read a small sample of the conversations as well.

You may find that the real issue is delayed quotes, unclear scope, missed callbacks, or a follow-up date that nobody owns. Fixing that step is more valuable than adding another message.
