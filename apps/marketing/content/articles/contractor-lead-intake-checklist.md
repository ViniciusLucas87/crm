A new inquiry does not need to become a long interview.

When a homeowner calls about a repair, an estimate, or a maintenance visit, they usually want to know whether you can help and when they will hear back. Your first job is to get enough information to make the return call useful.

That is different from collecting every possible detail before speaking with them. A short, consistent intake process protects the opportunity without making the customer do your planning for you.

## Start with the details you need to reply

Whether the inquiry arrived by phone, text, web form, or referral, record these basics first:

* The customer's name and best phone number
* The service address or at least the neighbourhood and city
* What they need help with, in their own words
* When the problem started or when they want the work done
* A good time and method for the return call

This is enough to decide who should respond and what they need to know before they do. It also prevents the familiar problem of finding a missed call later with no clue about the job.

## Ask one useful question about the work

The best next question depends on the trade. It should help you prepare for the conversation, not replace it.

For a plumbing request, you might ask where the leak is and whether the water can be shut off. For an HVAC inquiry, the customer might tell you whether the equipment has stopped completely or is making an unusual noise. For a renovation inquiry, the useful detail may be the room, the rough scope, and the desired start date.

Avoid promising a diagnosis or price by text when you do not have enough information. A simple note such as "kitchen sink leak, customer can talk after 3 p.m." is often more valuable than a long string of guesses.

## Confirm whether the request is in your service area and a fit

Before booking time, check the practical limits that protect both sides:

* Is the property in an area you serve?
* Is this work your business actually takes on?
* Is the requested timing realistic?
* Does the situation need a different type of help right now?

If a person describes an immediate danger, tell them to contact the appropriate emergency service or utility. Do not try to handle a safety-critical situation through an ordinary intake message.

For ordinary requests that are not a fit, a short direct answer is kinder than leaving the person waiting. You can explain that the job is outside your service area or specialty without recommending a company you cannot personally stand behind.

## Keep the first message short after a missed call

A missed-call text should invite a reply, not ask for a full application. The customer may be standing in a driveway, at work, or dealing with a problem at home.

This is a useful starting point:

> Hi, this is [Business Name]. Sorry we missed your call. Please reply with your name, what you need help with, and the area you are in. We will call you back as soon as we can.

The words should sound like your business. If you cannot offer same-day service, do not imply that you can. If your office is closed, say when someone will review the message rather than leaving the timing vague.

[Never Miss](/never-miss) is designed for this first step. When an eligible call goes unanswered, it sends the caller a clear reply and keeps the response together with the callback action. You still decide how to qualify the work and how to serve the customer.

## Give every inquiry an owner and a next action

Capturing details only helps if someone knows what to do next.

For each inquiry, record one owner and one dated next action. The owner may be the person returning the call, preparing an estimate, or confirming that the request is outside your scope. The next action might be "call before noon," "send estimate Tuesday," or "confirm site visit Friday."

This small habit matters more than a complex pipeline. A shared system, paper day sheet, or simple list can work at low volume if the team can see what is still open. Once calls, texts, and web inquiries are spread across several people, a shared inbox and follow-up tracking can make the work easier to see. ## Do not collect information you do not need

New customers may share more than is necessary when they want help quickly. Keep the first intake focused on the request. You generally do not need payment-card details, full personal history, or documents unrelated to the job just to decide whether to call back.

If photos, equipment labels, or documents would help, explain why you are asking and give the customer a simple way to share them. Keep those details with the job record rather than in a staff member's personal message thread.

## Review the checklist after a week

Try the checklist for a normal week, then look at the inquiries that were hardest to handle. Were callbacks delayed because the location was missing? Did the team need a clearer rule for after-hours requests? Were people asking for jobs you do not offer?

Adjust the checklist based on those real gaps. The goal is not to make intake perfect. It is to make the next conversation easier for the customer and more useful for your team.
