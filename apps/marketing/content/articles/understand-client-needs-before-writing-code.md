Clients do not come to us because they need more code.

They come because something in the business is slow, unclear, unreliable, or difficult to manage. Information is being copied between systems. A manager cannot see what is happening without asking three people. An employee spends Friday afternoon rebuilding the same report. A customer request disappears in an inbox.

Code may become part of the answer, but it is not the starting point.

Our first responsibility is to understand the work.

## The request is not always the need

A client may ask for a dashboard. That is a reasonable request, but it does not tell us enough to build the right thing.

Why is the dashboard needed? Who will use it? What decision should it help them make? Where does the information come from? Can that information be trusted? What happens when a number is missing or wrong?

If we skip those questions, we can produce a polished screen that does not improve the business.

The same is true when someone asks for a CRM, a mobile app, an AI assistant, or an automation. The requested product is often the client's best description of a problem they have been living with for a long time. We need to respect that request without treating it as a finished specification.

The real need may be narrower than the proposed system. It may also be more important.

## Start with the people doing the work

The owner understands the direction of the business. A manager understands the pressure on the team. The person doing the task every day understands every workaround.

We need all three views.

A process diagram prepared in a meeting room rarely captures what happens on a busy Tuesday morning. The real workflow includes exceptions, missing information, urgent requests, customer changes, and judgement calls that never appear in the official procedure.

That is why discovery should include the people closest to the work. We ask them to show us what they open, what they copy, what they check, and what they do when the normal process breaks.

This is not a formality before development. It is part of the engineering.

## Understand the current workflow before designing the next one

Before proposing software, we map the process from beginning to end.

We want to know:

- What starts the work?
- What information is needed at the beginning?
- Who handles each step?
- Where is information entered more than once?
- Where does the process wait?
- Which decisions require experience or judgement?
- What are the common exceptions?
- What must be recorded for the customer, the team, or compliance?
- How does everyone know the work is complete?

The purpose is not to produce a complicated document. The purpose is to make the operation visible enough that the client and the development team are talking about the same reality.

Our [delivery process](/process) begins with this operational review. It gives us a shared definition of the problem before scope, features, or estimates become commitments.

## Propose the smallest system that solves the right problem

Once the need is clear, we can decide what should change.

Sometimes the answer is custom software. Sometimes an existing tool can be configured properly. Sometimes two systems need a dependable integration. Sometimes the business needs to simplify the process before automating it.

We should be willing to recommend any of those outcomes.

When custom software is justified, the first release should focus on the part of the operation that creates the most useful improvement. It does not need every feature the business may want in three years. It needs to handle the important workflow reliably and give the team a better way to work now.

That creates something concrete for the client to review. It also replaces assumptions with real feedback before the system becomes larger and more expensive.

## Build around the client, not around our preferred technology

Developers have preferences. We know certain frameworks, platforms, and patterns well. Those preferences can help us deliver efficiently, but they should not control the client's solution.

The technology has to fit the business constraints.

A field team may need a mobile workflow that remains useful with poor connectivity. An office team may need a simple browser-based tool with strong search. A company with established accounting software may need an integration rather than a replacement. A regulated workflow may require approvals, history, and carefully controlled access from the beginning.

Our job is to choose and shape the technology around those needs. The client should not have to reorganize a sound operation just because a developer wanted to use a particular tool.

## Agree on what success means

"The software works" is not a useful measure on its own.

A successful project should improve something the client can recognize. That could mean reducing the time needed to prepare a report, removing duplicate entry, shortening an approval cycle, keeping job information in one place, or making follow-up work visible.

The measure should match the original problem.

Before development, we establish a practical baseline. The client can use our [Manual Work Cost Calculator](/free-tools/manual-work-cost-calculator) to estimate the current time and labour involved. The [Automation ROI Calculator](/free-tools/automation-roi-calculator) helps compare expected benefits with implementation and ongoing costs. These are planning tools, not promises, but they make assumptions visible.

After launch, we compare the new workflow with the old one. If the result is not improving the operation, more features are not automatically the answer. We return to the process and find out why.

## A client-needs checklist before development begins

For any custom software project, we should be able to answer these questions together:

1. What business problem are we trying to solve?
2. Who experiences the problem directly?
3. How does the work happen today, including exceptions?
4. Which information is required, and where does it come from?
5. What must the new system connect with or preserve?
6. What risks would make the solution unacceptable?
7. What is the smallest useful first release?
8. How will the client know the project improved the operation?

If those answers are vague, writing code will not make them clearer. It will only make the assumptions more expensive.

This checklist is also useful when comparing software vendors. A responsible proposal should show that the provider understands the current work, the desired outcome, the scope of the first release, and the limits of the recommendation.

## Trust is built before the first feature

Clients are sharing the part of their business that is not working as well as it should. That requires trust.

We earn that trust by listening carefully, explaining what we understand, admitting what we still need to learn, and avoiding a premature sales pitch. We should be able to describe the client's problem in their language before presenting our solution in ours.
