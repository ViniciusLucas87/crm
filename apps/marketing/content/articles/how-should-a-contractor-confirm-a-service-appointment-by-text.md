An appointment is not fully booked just because someone wrote it on a calendar.

The customer still needs to know who is coming, when to expect them, what to prepare, and how to speak up if the time no longer works. A short confirmation text answers those questions before they turn into a missed visit or a frustrated phone call.

This is not about sending more messages. It is about making one important message easy to understand and easy to act on.

## Send the confirmation when the time is actually agreed

Send a confirmation as soon as the customer and your business have agreed on the appointment. If someone on the team has only pencilled in a tentative time, do not word the message as if the booking is final.

For a service visit, the message should cover the practical details:

* Your business name and the person's name, if you know who is attending
* The date and arrival window, including the time zone if your work crosses regions
* The service address or a prompt to flag an incorrect address
* A short description of the planned work
* A simple way to confirm, change, or ask a question

Avoid a vague line such as "See you tomorrow." It leaves the customer to remember which company, which job, and what time. It also gives them no clear next step if something has changed.

## Keep the first message short enough to read quickly

Most people read a service text between other tasks. Put the appointment details first and do not bury the action you need.

Here is a simple starting point:

> Hi [First Name], this is [Business Name]. We have you booked for [service] at [address] on [day, date] between [arrival window]. Please reply YES to confirm, or reply here if you need to change the time. Thank you.

Use an arrival window you can reasonably meet. If your crew can only offer a four-hour window, say that plainly. If the customer must make space available, arrange parking, or secure a pet, include that one relevant instruction rather than sending a generic checklist.

The same principle applies when a call first goes unanswered. A clear [missed-call text](/blog/what-should-a-contractor-say-in-a-missed-call-text) can collect enough detail for a useful callback, while the appointment confirmation should only go out once the time is settled.

## Give customers an honest way to change the appointment

A reply path is more useful than a message that only says "do not reply." People may have a work conflict, an access issue, or a question about the scope. If they cannot tell you, the first sign of the problem may be an empty driveway.

Decide in advance who watches replies and what happens next. A small business can keep this simple:

1. One person owns the day’s confirmations and replies.
2. A reply asking for a change becomes a visible calendar or task update.
3. The new time is confirmed in writing before the old slot is released.
4. Any internal notes, such as gate codes or access instructions, stay in the business system rather than being copied into a group chat.

If you do not have someone monitoring messages after hours, say when you will review them. The goal is a reliable expectation, not an instant answer you cannot provide.

## Use a reminder only when it helps the customer prepare

For a visit scheduled well ahead, a short reminder can be useful the day before or on the morning of the appointment. It should repeat the essential detail, not introduce a surprise policy or an unrelated offer.

For example:

> Reminder from [Business Name]: your [service] visit is tomorrow, [date], between [arrival window]. Please reply here today if access or timing has changed.

Keep service messages separate from promotions. Canadian businesses that send commercial electronic messages need to consider consent, identification, and an unsubscribe mechanism. The CRTC explains those general requirements in its [CASL FAQ](https://crtc.gc.ca/eng/com500/faq500.htm). Rules can depend on the message and relationship, so this article is operational guidance, not legal advice. If you are unsure, get advice for your specific use.

## Make the calendar match what the customer was told

The customer-facing message and the internal calendar should agree on the same date, window, address, job type, and owner. A confirmation does not help if the office says 9 to 11 and the technician sees noon to 2.

Before the day begins, review three things:

* Which appointments need a confirmation or reminder
* Which replies need a human decision
* Which visits have travel, access, or equipment notes that affect the route

For businesses handling a lot of incoming calls, [Never Miss](/never-miss) is designed for a different but connected moment: an eligible unanswered call receives a reply and goes onto a callback list. Your team still decides whether to book the work and what appointment details to confirm.

## A confirmation is part of good service

Customers do not need a complicated booking system to feel looked after. They need accurate details, a way to raise a problem, and confidence that someone has seen their response.

Start with one plain confirmation template. Test it with the next few bookings. Then adjust the arrival window, reply wording, and reminder timing based on the questions customers actually ask. A small, dependable process is more useful than a long sequence no one maintains.
