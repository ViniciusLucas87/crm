For many service businesses, a phone is not just a phone. It holds customer names, addresses, gate instructions, photos, messages, job notes, and the history needed to finish the work well.

That is useful when a technician is on site. It is risky when the same details live only in a personal text thread, an unlocked device, or an account that nobody else can access when a staff member is away.

This is not a legal compliance checklist. It is a practical starting point for reducing avoidable exposure and keeping customer information available to the people who genuinely need it. For Canadian privacy obligations that apply to your situation, consult the [Office of the Privacy Commissioner of Canada's business privacy guidance](https://www.priv.gc.ca/en/privacy-topics/privacy-for-businesses/).

## Map the customer details that reach a phone

Start with a normal week of work. List the information that arrives through calls, texts, email, job software, photos, and shared files. A contractor might receive:

* Names, phone numbers, email addresses, and service addresses
* Photos of a home, equipment, or site issue
* Access instructions, appointment details, and notes about the work
* Invoices, receipts, warranty details, and contact history

Do not create a larger list just because you can. The first security improvement is often collecting only the information needed to quote, schedule, complete, or support the job.

## Protect the device before relying on an app

An unlocked phone can expose a surprising amount of customer information through message previews, photos, saved email, and browser sessions. Put the basics in place on every work device:

* Use a strong device passcode or biometric lock, with a short automatic lock time
* Turn on the device's supported update settings and install security updates promptly
* Enable the built-in option to locate, lock, or erase a lost device where it fits your business setup
* Review lock-screen previews so sensitive message details are not displayed to anyone who picks up the phone
* Use multi-factor authentication for business email, storage, and customer-management accounts

The Office of the Privacy Commissioner of Canada notes that mobile devices are easy to lose or steal and recommends a policy for taking personal information out of the office, along with up-to-date encryption for mobile devices. Its [mobile-device guidance](https://www.priv.gc.ca/en/privacy-topics/employers-and-employees/mobile-devices-and-online-services-at-work/02_05_d_46_dpd/) is a useful reference when you set your own rules.

## Keep customer work out of personal accounts where possible

Texting a customer from a personal number may be convenient in the moment, but it makes the conversation harder to hand off, review, or remove when someone leaves the business. The same problem happens when job photos or notes are stored only in a personal email account or photo library.

Where practical, give staff a business account and a shared place for customer records. The goal is not to watch every conversation. It is to make sure the business can continue serving the customer without needing access to an individual's personal phone.

For a small team, this can begin with a shared business inbox and clear rules for who owns each request. Our guide to [deciding whether your service business needs a shared inbox](/blog/does-my-small-service-business-need-a-shared-inbox) explains the ownership and handoff habits to establish before adding more software.

## Use a simple rule for photos, notes, and access details

Photos can make a diagnosis or repair discussion much easier, but they can also capture more than the job. Before staff request or share a photo, ask whether it is necessary and who needs to see it.

For example, a technician may need a picture of a water heater label. They probably do not need a broad photo of a customer's living space when a close-up will do. If an address, access code, or gate instruction is needed for a visit, keep it with the work record and avoid copying it into unrelated group chats.

Agree on a few plain rules:

1. Put job information in the approved business system as soon as practical.
2. Do not send customer details into a personal group chat or an account outside the business.
3. Do not ask customers to send payment-card details by ordinary text or email.
4. Remove downloaded job files and unnecessary photos from a device when they are no longer needed for the work.

The right tools will differ, but the rule is stable: access should match the job, and old copies should not quietly accumulate.

## Make access deliberate when people join or leave

Small teams often share access informally because everyone knows each other. That can work until a phone is replaced, a subcontractor finishes a project, or an employee leaves with an active session still on their device.

Keep a short list of the accounts and shared tools each role needs. When someone starts, give only the access required for their work. When their role changes or ends, remove their access, change shared credentials if necessary, and confirm that business records are still in the shared system.

This also makes everyday coverage easier. If one person has a day off, another team member can see the customer history and promised next action without borrowing a phone or asking the customer to start again.

## Test the workflow with a lost-phone scenario

You do not need to wait for a real loss to find the gaps. Pick one staff phone and ask:

* Could we lock or remove access from it if it went missing today?
* Which customer records would still be available to the business?
* Which personal accounts or text threads would leave the team without context?
* Would a replacement device receive only the access its user needs?

Write down the one or two gaps you find and fix those first. A clear passcode, a business account, and a consistent place for job notes will usually reduce more risk than a complicated policy nobody follows.

## Keep the customer record useful and appropriately private

Security should not make field work impossible. The aim is to give the right person the details they need while reducing unnecessary copies and unplanned access.
